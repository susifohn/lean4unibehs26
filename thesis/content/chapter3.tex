\section{Systems of Logic}

In classical logic, statements (=Propositions) are either true or false. No test or proof is needed whether a proposition is true or false. E.g. having a proposition $A$ the disjunction $A \lor \neg A$ is always true without showing which side of the disjunction holds. The Law of the Excluded Middle (LEM) holds. \\

On the other side, intuitionistic logic
rejects the LEM. Claiming $A \lor \neg A$ requires to prove at least one side of the disjunction namely $A$ or $\neg A$.\\
Proving $\exists \alpha : P(\alpha)$ requires a witness for $\alpha$ and an algorithm (or program) to compute $P(\alpha)$. A theorem in intutioniscit logic implies beeing a theorem in classical logic but not in the opposite directo. in general a tautology in classical logic is not a theorem in intutionistic logic.\\

\subsection{$\lambda$ Calculus}
Invented by Alonzo Church. He was also a Supervisor of Alain Turing. Church treated functions as black boxes with no
internal state, means pure functions. On the other side, Turing looked ad functions using the Turing Machine,
and states are crucial there. [Ref Graham Hutton, ambda Calculus - Computerphile].\\

In the $\lambda$ calculus three things exists: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item variables, like $x,y$
    \item a way of building functions with the $\lambda$ notation
    \item applying functions, using substitution
\end{itemize}
So with this,  we can encode True and False, NOT, AND and any function we want, including recursion. The $\lambda$ 
calculus and the Turing Machines are aequivalent systems regarding computability. What ever a TM can encode, the 
$\lambda$ calculus can do it as well and vice versa. This fact is known as the Church--Turing--Correspondence.
