\subsection{Some historical background}

The foundation of today's proof assistants, such as Rcoq and Lean 4, is built from several mathematical branches and it took decades before anyone saw its correspondence.

Arround 1920 Brouwer's intuitionism requires the presence of an constuctable evidence for any mathematical statement to be true. In 1930 Heyting gave the first axiomatisation of intuitionistic logic also known as constructive logic, where the law of excluded middle holds. Heyting and Kolmogorov then gave few years later an informal interpretation, treating proofs as constructions of evidences, known as the BHK-interpretation, explained in \ref{s:bhk_interpretation}.

Gentzen developed 1935 natural deduction for intuitionistic logic, see \ref{s:intuitionistic_logic}, to formalize mathematical reasoning.

In the same time, Church worked on the $\lambda$-calculus and Curry observed the first hint for the Curry-Howard-Correspondence, that types match axioms of implcational logic. A few years later Church extended to the simply typed $\lambda$-calculus.

Howard writes 1969  “The formulae-as-types notion of construction”, showing that Gentzen's natural deduction corresponds to the simply typed $lambda$-calculus. This is the date given for the correspondence itself. 

Years later in 1980 Howard showed the correspondence extends to the logical connectives $\land$ and $lor$ by extending the lambda calculus with pairs and disjoint sums. \cite{wadler2015propositions}








\section{Logical systems}
To make precise when a statement follows from other statements, a logical system is needed. The syntax defines how to construct well formed formulas. The semantics assigns meanings to formulas and determines when a formula is true or false in the system. In classical logic by truth valuations and in intuitionistic logic by Kripke models for example.  

\subsection{Classical propositional logic}
In classical logic, statements (=Propositions) are either true or false. No test or proof is needed whether a proposition is true or false. E.g. having a proposition $A$ the disjunction $A \lor \neg A$ is always true without showing which side of the disjunction holds. The Law of the Excluded Middle (LEM) holds.

\subsection{Intuitionistic propositional logic}
On the other side, intuitionistic logic IPC
rejects the LEM. Claiming $A \lor \neg A$ requires to prove at least one side of the disjunction namely $A$ or $\neg A$.

Proving $\exists \alpha : P(\alpha)$ requires a witness for $\alpha$ and an algorithm (or program) to compute $P(\alpha)$. A theorem in intuitionistic logic implies being a theorem in classical logic but not in the opposite direction. In general a tautology in classical logic is not a theorem in intuitionistic logic.

This matters because intuitionistic logic requires a constructive proof that an object exists and this construction suits as an algorithm or program to generate such object. The  Curry-Howard correspondence defines an isomorphism between such programs and proofs. As a consequence, a program in lean 4 or other proof assistants can be used as a proof and vice versa. \cite{web:intuitionistic_logic}

\subsection{The BHK interpretation of IPC}
\label{s:bhk_interpretation}
A proof of $A \to B$ is a method that transforms any proof of $A$ into a proof of $B$, and a proof of $A \wedge B$ is a pair of proofs. This reading already treats proofs as constructions, and so as something close to programs, although it was never made formal.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The $\lambda$-calculus}{The lambda-calculus}}
Invented by Alonzo Church 1933 \todo{add ref}. He was also a Supervisor of Alan Turing. Church treated functions as black boxes with no
internal state, means pure functions. On the other side, Turing looked at functions using the Turing Machine,
and states are crucial there. [Ref Graham Hutton, Lambda Calculus - Computerphile].

In the $\lambda$ calculus three things exists: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Variables, like $x,y$
    \item Abstraction, a way of building functions
    \item Application, applying functions to their arguments
\end{enumerate}


The $\lambda$-calculus consists of a precise notation to investigate functions.


\[
\underbrace{\lambda x_1.\lambda x_2}_{\text{arguments}}
.\underbrace{M}_{\substack{\text{function}\\[2pt]\text{body}}}
\]

where $x_i$, for $i \in \mathbb{N}_{>0}$ are the function arguments and $M$ is telling how the return value is calculated. For example the identity function
$$\lambda x.x$$

For function application, the input values are appended as follows

$$(
\lambda x.x) \;a
$$

returning $a$ in this case. Or in the case of two arguments $x,y$ and two input values $a,b$
$$
(\lambda x.\lambda y.M) a \: b
$$

The three parts of the $\lambda$-calculus variables, abstraction and application can be formulated as a grammar as follows
\[
M ::= x \mid \lambda x.M \mid M\,M
\]

With this simple notation,  we can encode True and False, NOT, AND and any function we want, including recursion and it follows that it is Turing complete. This fact is known as the Church–Turing thesis.\cite{web:lambda_calc}

\section{\texorpdfstring{The simply typed $\lambda$-calculus}{The simply typed lambda-calculus}}
assigns a type to every expression. Using the notation $(value : Type)$, we have for example

$$
(\lambda x.x : \alpha \to \alpha)
$$

or

$$
(\lambda x.\lambda y.y : \alpha \to \beta \to \beta)
$$

The type $\alpha \to \beta$ is the type of a function taking a value of type $\alpha$ and returning a value of type $\beta$. 

To infer the type of a valid term, we need rules. Let $\Gamma$ be the list of all assumptions, aka context. $\Gamma,x:\alpha$ denotes the extension of $\Gamma$ with the assumption that $x$ is of type $\alpha$.  The simply typed $\lambda$-calculus provides the following rules for variables, abstraction and application:

\[
\begin{array}{c}
\displaystyle
\frac{x : \alpha \in \Gamma}
     {\Gamma \vdash x : \alpha}
\quad\textsc{Var}
\\[2em]
\displaystyle
\frac{\Gamma, x : \alpha \vdash e : \beta}
     {\Gamma \vdash \lambda x.e : \alpha \to \beta}
\quad\textsc{Abs}
\\[2em]
\displaystyle
\frac{\Gamma \vdash f : \alpha \to \beta
      \qquad
      \Gamma \vdash a : \alpha}
     {\Gamma \vdash f\,a : \beta}
\quad\textsc{App}
\end{array}
\]
